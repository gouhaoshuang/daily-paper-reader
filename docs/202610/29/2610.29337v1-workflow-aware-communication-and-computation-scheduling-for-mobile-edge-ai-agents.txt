Workflow-Aware Communication and Computation
Scheduling for Mobile Edge AI Agents
Xingqiu He, Chaoqun You, Feng Wang, Zihan Chen, Shuai Wang, Jihong Park
Abstract—AI agents are increasingly deployed in mobile edge
environments to autonomously handle diverse tasks through
multi-step workflows. Unlike conventional dependency-aware
tasks with predefined dependency structures, agent workflows are
dynamically generated based on intermediate execution results.
To minimize the weighted sum of job completion latencies, this
paper models partially revealed agent workflows using dynamic
workflow graphs and formulates an online scheduling problem
that jointly optimizes computation placement, wireless resource
block allocation, and edge computing resource allocation. To
address the uncertainty of future workflow evolution, we first
transform the weighted completion time objective into an equiv-
alent per-slot holding cost and characterize the optimal policy
through a Bellman equation. We then construct a tractable look-
ahead cost-to-go surrogate based on the revealed workflow and
the estimated remaining latencies of active jobs. The proposed
scheduling algorithm leverages the surrogate to determine com-
putation placement and uses the resulting operation criticality to
allocate wireless and edge computing resources online. Simula-
tion results show that the proposed algorithm outperforms the
benchmark schemes across diverse scenarios.
Index Terms—Agent workflow, scheduling algorithm, edge
computing
I. INTRODUCTION
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have
enabled AI agents to reason, take actions, and invoke exter-
nal tools [1]–[4]. Such agents are increasingly deployed in
interactive and mobile environments [5]–[7] to support appli-
cations requiring both environmental awareness and intelligent
decision-making. Unlike a single model query, an agent job is
typically executed through a multi-step workflow consisting of
operations such as sensing, preprocessing, data transmission,
model inference, and tool invocation [8]. These communi-
cation and computation operations often exhibit dependency
constraints, where an operation becomes ready only after all
its predecessors are completed.
Different from conventional dependency-aware tasks with
known dependency structures, an agent workflow is dynami-
cally generated based on intermediate execution results. Con-
sequently, an online scheduler can only observe the revealed
portion of the workflow and must allocate resources before
future operations become available. Moreover, executing the
entire workflow locally is often infeasible due to the lim-
ited computational capability of mobile devices. Although
edge and cloud computing provide more powerful computa-
tional resources, offloading operations introduces additional
transmission delays and resource contention. Therefore, the
job completion latency is jointly determined by computation
placement and the allocation of shared communication and
computing resources [9], [10].
To improve user experience, it is essential to optimize
resource allocation to minimize the completion latency of
agent jobs. However, designing an online scheduling algorithm
for such workflows faces the following three challenges. First,
the scheduler must evaluate the long-term impact of each
decision based on an incomplete workflow, without knowing
future operations and their dependencies. Second, communi-
cation and computation are tightly coupled through workflow
dependencies: wireless transmission determines when down-
stream computation operations become ready, while compu-
tation placement determines both their processing rates and
the additional data transmissions required along the workflow.
Third, concurrent jobs compete for shared wireless and edge
computing resources, whose availability varies over time due
to channel fluctuations and background traffic. These factors
make computation placement, wireless resource allocation, and
edge computing allocation highly coupled.
In this paper, we investigate joint communication and
computation scheduling for dynamically revealed mobile edge
agent workflows. We model each partially revealed workflow
as a dynamic workflow graph and formulate an online schedul-
ing problem that minimizes the weighted sum of job comple-
tion latencies. To characterize how current decisions affect this
objective, we first transform the weighted completion-latency
objective into an equivalent per-slot holding cost and derive
the corresponding Bellman equation. Since the exact cost-to-
go cannot be evaluated due to unknown future workflow and
resource states, we construct a tractable surrogate based on
the revealed workflow and the estimated remaining latency
of each active job. The proposed surrogate assigns smaller
weights to holding costs predicted to occur farther in the
future and incorporates a correction term to capture the state
transitions caused by operation completion. Based on the
resulting operation criticality, the proposed scheduler jointly
determines computation placement and resource allocation for
communication and computation operations in each slot.
The main contributions are summarized as follows.
• We introduce a dynamic workflow graph to model agent
workflows whose operations and dependencies are grad-
ually revealed during execution. Based on this model, we
formulate an online scheduling problem that jointly op-
timizes computation placement, wireless RB allocation,
and edge computing resource allocation to minimize the
weighted sum of job completion latencies.

II. RELATED WORK
Dependency-Aware Task Scheduling. Scheduling tasks
with precedence dependencies has been extensively studied in
heterogeneous and edge computing. HEFT assigns the opera-
tions of a known DAG to heterogeneous processors according
to their upward ranks [11]. PEFT further uses an optimistic
cost table to incorporate downstream execution costs into
task ranking and processor selection [12]. In edge computing
networks, computation offloading further couples task exe-
cution with wireless and computing resource allocation [9].
Dependency-aware studies optimize offloading and scheduling
across device, edge, and cloud resources [13], [14]. Recent
online approaches further update task priorities as multiple
applications arrive and resource conditions change [15]. These
methods account for resource heterogeneity and workflow
dependencies, and some adapt their decisions online. However,
the complete set of operations and dependencies of each
admitted application is available before scheduling begins.
Resource Scheduling for Edge AI. Resource scheduling
for edge AI has mainly focused on accelerating DNN infer-
ence under limited communication and computing resources.
Neurosurgeon [16], JointDNN [17], and Edge AI [10] partition
a DNN between mobile devices and infrastructure resources
according to computation and communication conditions.
DADS further adapts DNN partitioning between edge and
cloud resources to changing network conditions [18]. Beyond
device–server partitioning, CoEdge distributes inference work-
loads across heterogeneous edge devices according to their
computing capabilities and network conditions [19]. Jellyfish
jointly selects data and DNN adaptations and batches infer-
ence requests under dynamic wireless latency [20], whereas
Ekya allocates edge GPU resources between inference and
model retraining [21]. These studies provide effective resource
scheduling mechanisms for AI inference, but their operation
graphs are determined by fixed DNN architectures or prede-
fined service pipelines. Autellix instead targets dynamically
generated LLM-agent programs and introduces ATLAS, which
prioritizes programs based on their attained service [22].
In contrast, agent workflows may interleave communication,
heterogeneous computation, and tool invocation operations
that are gradually revealed during execution.

III. SYSTEM MODEL AND PROBLEM FORMULATION
In this section, we first introduce the dynamic agent work-
flow model and the wireless edge resource model, then for-
mulate the online workflow-aware scheduling problem.
A. Dynamic Agent Workflow Model
As shown in Fig. 1, we consider a single-cell wireless edge
system consisting of a finite set D of wireless devices, a base
station (BS) co-located with an edge server, and a remote cloud
accessible through backhaul. The devices in D may include
mobile terminals, cameras, sensors, or other wireless endpoints
involved in agent execution. Multiple agents are deployed
across different devices to support diverse applications, and
each agent initiates a job when it needs to handle a task.
The time horizon is divided into equal-length slots, and
t ∈{0, 1, 2, . . .} denotes the slot index. We consider a finite
set J of jobs that arrive over time. For each job j, let aj and
ωj denote its arrival slot and priority weight, respectively.
We assume a central scheduler at the BS to orchestrate agent
job execution. During each slot, newly generated workflow op-
erations and their dependencies are reported to the scheduler.
The scheduler also obtains wireless channel information from
the BS, monitors the available edge computing capacity, and
tracks the execution state of the workflow. Based on this in-
formation, the scheduler jointly optimizes the communication
and computation resources allocated to the agents.

IV. ALGORITHM DESIGN
The main difficulty in solving (4) is that the completion
time of a job depends on future workflow operations, resource
availability, and channel conditions, which are unknown when
the current scheduling decision is made. To address this issue,
we first transform the weighted completion time objective into
an equivalent per-slot holding-cost objective. Based on this
reformulation, the optimal online policy can be characterized
by a Bellman equation. However, directly solving the Bellman
equation is computationally impractical because the future
workflow and resource states are unknown. We therefore
construct a tractable cost-to-go surrogate based on the revealed
workflow and the estimated remaining latency of each active
job. We use this surrogate to compare computation-placement
actions and derive the criticality of each ready operation for
shared wireless and edge-computing resource allocation.

a decision in slot t, the value of Fj is unknown, as it
depends on unrevealed workflow operations, future resource
availability, channel conditions, and subsequent scheduling
decisions. Therefore, the original completion time objective
is difficult to optimize directly in an online setting.

V. PERFORMANCE EVALUATION
A. Simulation Setup
In this section, we evaluate the proposed scheduling algo-
rithm against three benchmarks under various settings. Each
job is represented by a hidden DAG, while the scheduler
only observes the operations revealed through corresponding
reveal events. A default workflow contains 12–40 operations,
with a depth of 4–12 and a maximum fan-out of four, and
selects communication endpoints from a pool of 20 devices.
Jobs arrive according to a Poisson process, and their weights
are sampled from {1, 2, 4} with probabilities {0.6, 0.3, 0.1}.
Communication payloads and computation workloads follow
bounded log-normal distributions.
Each slot lasts 10 ms. The number of RBs available for
agent traffic in each direction is independently and uniformly
sampled from [5, 20]. The uplink and downlink transmission
rates are 100 and 200 kbit per RB-slot, respectively. For each
device and transmission direction, the achievable rate is scaled
by an independent log-normal channel factor with a coefficient
of variation of 0.1. The edge server provides 3–6 GFLOP per
slot, while the cloud execution is assumed to be contention-
free, providing 12 GFLOP per slot, with a one-way delay of
20 ms and 100/200-Mbps uplink/downlink backhaul.
The tail-latency predictor is trained using 1,000 indepen-
dently generated workflows. It estimates the remaining latency
using the median latency indexed by task family, operation
type, and operation identifier, with the global training-set mean
used as a fallback. The reference communication and compu-
tation rates are updated using exponential moving averages
with a coefficient of 0.2, and the look-ahead parameter is set
to ζ = 25. The default arrival rate is set to 4 jobs/s, and each
experiment contains 300 jobs. To mitigate randomness, each
data point is averaged over 10 independent random seeds.
We compare the proposed scheduler with WSRPT [23],
weighted ATLAS (W-ATLAS) adapted from the attained-
service policy in Autellix [22], and myopic equal-share
scheduling. For each revealed operation, W-ATLAS assigns
a priority proportional to the job weight divided by one plus
the completed attained service along its revealed predecessor
path. It adopts the same myopic placement rule as WSRPT.
B. Numerical Results
Fig. 2 demonstrates the end-to-end scheduling performance
under different traffic intensities. As the job arrival rate in-
creases from 1 to 6 jobs/s, the weighted mean completion
latency of Proposed increases from 3.08 to 9.23 s, whereas
that of WSRPT increases from 3.08 to 12.44 s. At 6 jobs/s,
Proposed reduces the latency by 25.58%, 80.79%, and 52.86%
relative to WSRPT, W-ATLAS, and Myopic, respectively. The
widening performance gap under heavier traffic is consistent
with the behavior of the baselines, which distribute service
across active workflows without explicitly considering future
impacts. Specifically, W-ATLAS may consistently favor newly
arrived operations, while Myopic equal sharing ignores job
weights and downstream workflow dependencies.
Fig. 3 examines whether the benefit of joint scheduling
persists as the system shifts between communication- and
computation-limited regimes. WSRPT is the strongest non-
Proposed baseline in every cell of the 5 × 5 grid. With
five available RBs, the improvement of Proposed increases
from 4.13% at 0.5× edge capacity to 6.27% at 2× edge
capacity, whereas with twenty RBs it is only 0.25%–1.56%.

Fig. 4 quantifies the online scheduling overhead as the num-
ber of active revealed operations increases. The plot contains
eight bins from 1–2 through 33–48 operations, with at least
1,000 scheduler calls per algorithm in every retained bin. For
Proposed, the mean runtime increases from 0.057 ms in the
smallest bin to 1.594 ms in the largest. At 33–48 operations,
the corresponding means are 0.482 ms for WSRPT, 0.647 ms
for W-ATLAS, and 0.211 ms for Myopic. The higher overhead
of Proposed is consistent with its repeated evaluation of branch
latencies and surrogate costs after each virtual completion.
Nevertheless, its maximum runtime remains acceptable, and
the runtime of all algorithms can be further reduced through
implementations in more efficient programming languages and
on more powerful hardware platforms.
Fig. 5 and Fig. 6 illustrate the scheduling performance
under different numbers of participating devices and work-
flow sizes, respectively. As the number of devices increases,
the weighted mean completion latency of WSRPT remains
between 109.08% and 115.15% relative to the proposed sched-
uler across the evaluated range. The latency ratio of W-ATLAS
decreases from 503.38% with two devices to 226.10% with
ten devices, while that of Myopic decreases from 144.66% to
121.69%. Thus, the proposed scheduler consistently achieves
the lowest latency across all device scales. As the workflow
size increases, the latency ratios of all baselines decrease:
WSRPT decreases from 125.25% to 113.48%, W-ATLAS from
725.37% to 542.81%, and Myopic from 220.76% to 163.69%,
relative to the proposed scheduler. These results show that
Proposed maintains the lowest latency as either the number
of participating devices or the workflow size increases, with
WSRPT remaining the closest benchmark.
Fig. 7 presents the statistics of weighted completion latency.
The weighted mean latency of Proposed is 4.50 s, compared
with 4.81 s for WSRPT, 22.24 s for W-ATLAS, and 7.30 s for
Myopic. Although WSRPT achieves a slightly lower weighted
median latency than Proposed (3.54 versus 3.65 s), Proposed
reduces the weighted p95 latency from 12.22 to 10.28 s
and the weighted p99 latency from 32.61 to 26.23 s. This
indicates that the advantage of Proposed over WSRPT mainly
appears in the upper tail of the latency distribution rather than
around the median. Myopic exhibits higher weighted mean,
median, and p95 latencies than Proposed, but achieves a lower
weighted p99 latency of 15.15 s. Therefore, a lower weighted
mean latency does not necessarily imply consistent superiority
across all tail quantiles.
VI. CONCLUSION
This paper studied the joint scheduling of communication
and computation for mobile edge AI agents whose workflows
are revealed online. By reformulating weighted completion
time as a per-slot holding cost, we developed a workflow-
aware scheduler that uses a look-ahead surrogate with com-
pletion correction to jointly determine computation placement
and wireless and edge resource allocation. Simulation results
show that the proposed algorithm achieves the most significant
reduction in weighted mean completion latency under heavy
traffic and strong radio contention, while incurring limited
runtime overhead.
