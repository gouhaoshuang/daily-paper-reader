Abstract
Recursive large language model (LLM) agents often invoke a
large host model after decomposition has reduced the problem
to grounding a leaf task into one executable action. Our traces
show that this last-mile grounding consumes 22.9–44.5%
of host-model tokens. We introduce LeafSkill, an external
action-grounding layer that reuses local task–action experience
without taking over recursive planning. LeafSkill separates
recall from execution: semantic retrieval proposes task–action
templates, deterministic binding instantiates currently admissi-
ble actions, and a lightweight controller selects a candidate or
abstains. The controller cannot generate free-form commands,
and abstention returns control to the host. With the same
ReCAP planner and Qwen3.5-27B host, LeafSkill reduces
host tokens on jointly solved tasks by 23.1% on Robotouille
and 41.3% on ALFWorld, each averaged over all nine cross-
run pairings of three independent repeats; an independent
Robotouille set yields a 14.4% reduction. Mean observed suc-
cess is numerically higher for LeafSkill in both environments.
A disjoint growth study expands the library from 446 to 588
skills without selector retraining or test trajectories; on tasks
solved by all systems, host-token savings deepen from 18.3%
to 25.6%. Ablations show that retrieval supplies paraphrase
coverage, binding enforces executability, and learned selection
controls when reuse is appropriate. Leaf-level action grounding
therefore reduces expensive host inference while preserving
recursive planning and recovery.

Introduction
Recursive LLM agents tackle long-horizon tasks by decom-
posing goals into progressively smaller task nodes (Yao et al.
2023b,a; Hu et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Yet decompo-
sition does not eliminate expensive host-model inference.
Even after a leaf task has become explicit, the agent may
still invoke the same large model merely to translate that
task into one action accepted by the current environment. For
example, once the planner has produced “pick up onion1 from
board1,” the remaining decision is no longer how to solve the
overall goal, but how to ground this local instruction into an
executable command.
We call this operation leaf-level action grounding: mapping
an already specified task node to one currently admissible
environment action, without altering decomposition, parent

Reusing these decisions is not equivalent to exact-string
caching. Semantically equivalent tasks may be expressed by
different phrases, while similar phrases may encode different
intents. Retrieved arguments may be absent from the current
state, and the same task may ground to different actions
under different observations. Effective reuse must therefore
solve four distinct problems: semantic recall, state-dependent
instantiation, candidate disambiguation, and safe abstention.
We introduce LeafSkill, an external action-grounding layer
for recursive LLM agents. Its frozen library stores canonical-
task/action-template pairs, each of which we call a skill;
multiple skills may share one canonical task key. Given a leaf
task, semantic retrieval recalls related templates, deterministic
binding instantiates them with entities from the current state
and removes inadmissible actions, and a lightweight controller
selects among the remaining request-local candidates or
abstains. The controller cannot generate free-form commands,
and abstention returns control to the host without changing
agent state. LeafSkill thus delegates only the final choice
within a finite, state-bound action set, while recursive planning,
recovery, and goal completion remain entirely host-owned.

Related Work
Recursive agent backbones.
ReAct interleaves reasoning
with interaction (Yao et al. 2023b), while Tree of Thoughts
searches over intermediate reasoning states (Yao et al. 2023a).
HiAgent manages long-horizon context through hierarchical
working memory (Hu et al. 2025), and ReCAP represents
goals, subtasks, and feedback in a recursive task tree (Zhang
et al. 2025). These approaches primarily improve planning,
context management, and recovery. LeafSkill is complemen-
tary: it operates only after an environment adapter exposes
an eligible leaf task, without taking over decomposition or
tree-state control. We instantiate this boundary with ReCAP
as the recursive host.
Experience and skill reuse.
Agents reuse prior experience
as verbal feedback or distilled lessons (Shinn et al. 2023;
Zhao et al. 2024), executable programs or workflows (Wang
et al. 2024, 2025), and retrieved demonstrations (Kim et al.
2024). RAG externalizes evidence from parametric genera-
tion (Lewis et al. 2020). Agent-oriented work includes Ex-
pRAG, which retrieves trajectories into an action-generating
context (Ferraz et al. 2026), and SkillRet, which benchmarks
skill retrieval (Cho, Kang, and Kim 2026). Recent systems
further structure experience as dependency-aware skill doc-
uments or executable multi-step graphs: Graph of Skills

LeafSkill differs in both reuse granularity and delegated
authority (Table 1). It retrieves task–action templates, binds
them to the current state, and permits the local controller to
choose only among the resulting admissible actions or abstain.
Candidate identities are request-local rather than members of
a fixed global output vocabulary, so compatible skills can be
added without changing the selector’s output space.
Constrained action grounding.
Affordance-aware meth-
ods combine language-model proposals with external fea-
sibility signals (Ahn et al. 2022). SCLPlan uses PDDL-
defined symbolic oversight to constrain goal and plan con-
struction (Byrd et al. 2025), while CRANE constrains
language-model generation through grammar-constrained
decoding (Banerjee et al. 2025). LeafSkill targets a narrower
boundary: retrieval proposes reusable templates, determinis-
tic binding converts them into currently admissible actions,
and the learned controller selects only within that finite set or
abstains. It therefore constrains one leaf-level action without
modifying the host planner or its decoding process.
Cost-aware model routing.
Cost-aware systems select or
sequence models for a complete request (Chen, Zaharia, and
Zou 2024; Ong et al. 2025). LeafSkill does not route the
recursive prompt: the host first determines the leaf task, and
the local controller chooses only among state-bound actions
before control returns to the same host.

Overview
LeafSkill has an offline construction stage and an online
grounding stage. Offline, host trajectories are converted into
canonical task–action templates and selector supervision.
Online, LeafSkill is invoked only at adapter-eligible leaf
tasks: it retrieves related templates, binds them to the current
admissible action set, and either selects one action or abstains.

Leaf-Level Delegation Boundary
Definition (leaf-level grounding). Leaf-level grounding maps an
already specified task node to one action in the environment’s
current admissible set, without modifying task decomposition, a
parent’s plan, or task-completion state.

Here ⊥is a state-preserving abstention: no delegated action
is executed, and the same node returns to the host. The
environment adapter determines where delegation may be
attempted; the selector determines whether a bound candidate

Design principles.
Narrow delegation keeps decomposi-
tion, recovery, and completion with the host. Retrieval for
recall proposes semantically related experience but cannot
authorize execution. Binding for admissibility instantiates
current entities and removes commands outside At. Selec-
tion with abstention resolves the remaining ambiguity while
preserving the host path when local evidence is insufficient.
Retrieve–Bind–Select
Each skill s ∈L is a canonical-task/action-template pair
with observed surface forms and provenance. Multiple skills
may share one canonical task key, preserving state-dependent
one-to-many actions. For an eligible request, retrieval and
binding construct

Semantic retrieval.
The retriever ranks canonical task keys
against τt and returns the top-k keys above similarity threshold
γ. All templates associated with those keys proceed to binding.
Retrieval is recall oriented: it may propose multiple related
intents, but a retrieved template alone cannot execute.
State-bound binding.
The binder instantiates typed tem-
plate slots with entities from the current request and retains
only concrete commands that exactly match an action in At.
It merges duplicate skill–action pairs, producing the finite
candidate set Ct.
Local selection and abstention.
Candidates receive
request-local identifiers and are shuffled before presenta-
tion to the selector. The selector chooses one identifier or
⊥; it cannot generate a free-form command. The selected
identifier is resolved through the request-local mapping and
checked against At again before execution. The selector is
not a policy over the full admissible set: retrieval and binding
define its request-local support, and an empty support returns
control to the host. Algorithm 1 gives the complete online
procedure.

Selector and Deterministic Guards
The selector receives only the task node, local observation,
admissible actions, and localized candidate descriptions. It
does not receive the root goal, ancestor path, remaining plan,
persistent skill identifiers, or historical support statistics. All
candidates surviving binding are visible, and request-local
identifiers are randomized so that the selector must read
candidate content rather than memorize a global label.
Empty retrieval, empty binding, explicit abstention, an
unknown identifier, or a failed final admissibility check returns
⊥. A skill associated with a failed local execution may be
masked for the rest of that episode, but the mask resets between
episodes and never changes the library. Table 3 summarizes

Library and Supervision Construction
A library record is extracted when the host proposes a
task node and subsequently executes an environment ac-
tion. Canonicalization converts the pair into a task key and
an action template. This is local attribution, not a claim that
the action belongs to a globally optimal trajectory.
Skill libraries.
Robotouille uses 200 group-A source trees;
199 yield at least one pair, for 11,483 task–action instances.
Canonicalization produces 413 task keys and 446 skills. After
controller training, the growth condition unions this library
with pairs from 200 group-B ReCAP trees and deduplicates
canonical task–action templates. The expanded library con-
tains 527 keys and 588 skills, adding 114 keys and 142
skills without controller updates. Group C is never used
for construction, model selection, or adaptation. ALFWorld
uses successful valid_seen source episodes, retaining
1,091 logged instances (1,089 unique executable contexts)
and producing 226 task keys and 228 skills.

Compatible post-training additions.
New skills are com-
patible when they share the existing action grammar and typed
slots, use the same retriever, bind to an exact member of At,
and follow the selector’s request-local description schema.
Request-local identifiers remove a fixed label vocabulary but
do not supply new binders or action semantics. The growth
experiments therefore test read-time incorporation within one
interface and template family.
Implementation Details
The retriever is BGE-small-en-v1.5 (Xiao et al. 2023), and
the selector is Qwen3-1.7B (Yang et al. 2025). Libraries and
selector parameters are frozen during evaluation, with no
online memory updates.

Metrics.
Success is computed over every scheduled episode,
with terminated episodes counted as failures. Success differ-
ences use two-sided exact McNemar tests on all task pairs.
Host efficiency is computed on tasks solved by both systems
in a run pairing because failed long-horizon trajectories have
heterogeneous stopping behavior, so their token counts con-
flate grounding cost with failure timing. We report reductions
in Qwen3.5-27B prompt-plus-completion tokens and calls,
termed paired-success host cost; reporting full-set success
alongside this metric prevents apparent savings from arising
mechanically from earlier or more frequent failures. When
both systems have three repeats, the reported reduction is
the arithmetic mean of the nine cross-run pairings; tasks are
intersected and the cost ratio is computed separately within
each cell before averaging. A single-run ablation is paired
with all three ReCAP repeats and reported as the mean of
those three reductions. Group-C cost intervals use task-level
bootstrap 95% CIs.
Controls and training.
End-to-end methods share task
identifiers, model services, context limits, decoding settings,
and termination rules. Retrieval uses k = 5 and similarity
threshold γ = 0.70 throughout, with no test-set hyperpa-
rameter search. ALFWorld uses ten round-robin shards and
a 49-action limit; every run records all 134 unseen games.
Controller tokens are reported separately because the 1.7B
and 27B tokenizers differ. Only LoRA parameters of the
1.7B selector are updated; the host, planner, retriever, and
binder remain frozen. Each main-system SFT stage uses one
epoch of rank-16 LoRA, batch size 1, gradient accumulation
16, learning rate 2 × 10−5, and maximum length 1,536 on
one A800 80GB GPU; the context-augmented input-scope
diagnostic uses maximum length 1,792.

Retrieval and binding.
Exact reuse of raw task–action
pairs changes paired host tokens by only 0.20% on average.
Semantic retrieval over the same raw pairs yields an 8.08%
reduction, while state-bound templates with direct Top-1
execution raise the reduction to 13.08% (Table 8). Canonical-
Exact with the SFT selector reaches 67.0% success and
29.60% mean token reduction on the original benchmark
wording. Its coverage falls from 99.5% on original reference
actions to 0% under both controlled rewrites; semantic Top-5
retains 96.0% lexical and 97.0% syntactic coverage (Table 9).
Semantic retrieval therefore supplies the paraphrase coverage
used by the default system.
Selection and abstention.
On the 100 tasks solved by both
BGE Top-1 and LeafSkill R2, Top-5 selection provides a
further 14.9% host-token reduction. SFT raises mixed-rank
accuracy from 81.9% to 93.3%. End to end, Base reaches
113/200 success and 27.01% mean token reduction; SFT
averages 62.17% success and 23.09% reduction, and is 9.0
points more successful in the direct R2 comparison (p =
.0198). Thus, the selector calibrates when and which bound
candidate to execute. In the all-negative OOD diagnostic, SFT
returns FALLBACK_RECAP in only 7.5% of cases. Because
binding is bypassed, this measures controller-only fallback
rather than online abstention and motivates deterministic
binding before selection. Adding true planner context yields
90.7% mixed-rank accuracy versus 93.3% with the local
interface.

Discussion and Conclusion
Efficiency and growth.
LeafSkill replaces repeated 27B
leaf-grounding calls with a finite local choice while leaving
planning and recovery to the host. Retrieval supplies para-
phrase coverage, binding enforces admissibility, and selection
resolves candidates. Request-local identifiers also support
compatible additions: expanding the library from 446 to 588
skills deepens fixed-set host-token savings from 18.3% to
25.6% without retraining.
Scope.
LeafSkill assumes recurring parameterizable leaves,
a discrete admissible-action interface, and an environment
adapter. New action grammars require corresponding adapters
and binders, while open-ended or continuous actions may
need richer executable-action interfaces. The present evidence
covers one planner, one host, two environments, and one
selector family.
Overall, LeafSkill shows that recurring leaf grounding can
be separated from recursive reasoning without replacing the
host planner. Across Robotouille and ALFWorld, its retrieve–
bind–select layer reduces Qwen3.5-27B host tokens by 14.4–
41.3%, while compatible library growth increases savings
without selector retraining. These results support state-bound
action reuse as a practical complement to recursive LLM
planning rather than a replacement for it.
