## DAC-Bench: A Decision-Aware Benchmark for Compositional Mobile GUI Tasks

Abstract 

Mobile GUI agents powered by LMMs can perceive screens and follow instructions, yet existing benchmarks largely target short, lin-ear workflows and step-level accuracy, offer-ing limited insight into long-horizon planning and decision-making under branching struc-tures. We present DAC-Bench, a decision-aware benchmark with compositional tasks comprising 830 episodes and 11,345 action steps across 35 applications on Android and iOS. Tasks are organized into Sequential, Con-junctive, Conditional, and Hierarchical struc-tures, reflecting real-world multi-step and branching interaction patterns. To complement standard step-level evaluation, we introduce weighted longest common subsequence to cap-ture length-sensitive progress and decision ac-curacy for branch correctness. Evaluations across 7 diverse agents show substantial per-formance degradation compared to prior bench-marks, with success rates dropping below 5% on 6–8 step tasks and branch accuracy aver-aging 38%, highlighting challenges in condi-tional decision-making. By exposing these fail-ure modes, DAC-Bench provides a challeng-ing and diagnostic benchmark for advancing decision-aware mobile GUI agents. Our code and dataset are available at: https://github. com/YuqingZhangMirror12/DAC-Bench .

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) have significantly improved capabilities in visual understanding (Yin et al., 2024), com-plex instruction following (Wen et al., 2024a; Qin et al., 2024), and multi-step task planning (Li et al., 2024a), enabling a wide range of autonomous agents. Within this landscape, spanning tool-using systems and embodied agents (Fan et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2023), graphical user interface (GUI) agents have emerged as a practical paradigm. By

perceiving screen contents and executing struc-tured actions, GUI agents can operate existing soft-ware environments (Liu et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2024a; Hu et al., 2025). In particular, mobile GUI agents (Chai et al., 2025; Jiang et al., 2025) have received growing attention due to their wide rele-vance to common mobile interactions and the ubiq-uity of smartphones, motivating increasing efforts to automate routine tasks through simulated user interactions (Ye et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2025). Systematic evaluation of mobile GUI agents is essential for assessing current capabilities and ex-posing limitations. Existing benchmarks, however, typically focus on perceptual grounding and linear instruction execution. Early work emphasizes GUI element grounding and visual understanding (Li et al., 2025), while later benchmarks extend to single-step or short multi-step instruction follow-ing (Rawles et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024b), and increasing instruction length to probe task diffi-culty (Li et al., 2024b). Other efforts explore cross-app interactions (Lu et al., 2024) or broaden cover-age to non-English interfaces and localized appli-cation ecosystems (Zhang et al., 2025c). Despite these advances, current mobile GUI benchmarks still exhibit three major limitations. 1) 

Limited instruction diversity. Most benchmarks expand tasks through template substitution or lin-ear concatenation of short instructions (Chen et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2024). Such construction often yields semantically repetitive tasks and reflects bi-ases by a small set of annotators’ brainstorming. In addition, action descriptions often reuse similar phrasing and constraints, limiting the coverage of user’s instruction diversity. 2) Overly sequential workflows. Existing datasets typically formalize tasks as linear step-by-step chains with minimal constraints, overlooking decision-aware behaviors involving conditional branching, fallback strategies, or multi-condition filtering. In practice, users com-monly adapt their interaction strategies based on

intermediate outcomes (e.g., “purchasing a high-priced item if the cheaper one is out of stock” ,or “adjusting travel plans based on weather fore-casts” ). 3) Limited evaluation protocols. Current metrics primarily assess step-level correctness or overall task success. While effective for short tasks, these measures do not adequately reflect progress in longer workflows or accuracy at decision points as shown in Fig 1. These limitations hinder systematic evaluation of mobile GUI agents in long-horizon, decision-aware, and cross-context scenarios. To address these limitations, we introduce DAC-Bench , a benchmark for evaluating mobile GUI agents on long-horizon and decision-aware tasks. We develop a human-LLM collaborative atomic in-struction and constraint pipeline that composes diverse atomic actions into Graph-structured Compositional Tasks spanning Sequential, Con-junctive, Conditional and Hierarchical structures. A mixture-of-generators construction strategy with strict manual quality control ensures task exe-cutability and semantic diversity. DAC-Bench con-sists of 830 task episodes with 11,345 actions steps across 35 applications on both Android and iOS platforms, with an average task depth of 13.3 steps. To complement existing evaluation protocols, we introduce decision-sensitive metrics that weight long-horizon progress and explicitly assess branch-ing accuracy, enabling finer-grained diagnosis be-yond step-level exact match or overall success rate. These components make DAC-Bench a challeng-ing benchmark for identifying agents limitations in 

compositional, decision-aware settings that bet-ter approximate real-world mobile interaction patterns. Our key contributions are as follows: 1. We release DAC-Bench , the first benchmark for long-horizon, decision-aware mobile GUI tasks motivated by real-world user interaction patterns. 2. Principled construction pipeline: we de-sign a collaborative pipeline that expands atomic instructions with constraints and composes them into graph-structured compositional tasks, balanc-ing executability and semantic diversity. 3. Decision-sensitive evaluation: we augment standard step-level and success-based metrics with complementary measures that weight long-horizon steps and explicitly evaluate branching accuracy. 4. Comprehensive evaluation: we conduct systematic experiments across 7 general-purpose, mobile-specialized, and reasoning agents, provid-ing diagnostic insights into model limitations. 

2 DAC-Bench 

2.1 Task Formulation 

The DAC-Bench dataset consists of a set of k

mobile GUI task episodes T = {T1, T 2, ..., T k}

generated from m candidate mobile applications 

A = {A1, A 2, ..., A m}. As illustrated in Fig 2, for each application Ai, we define an atomic instruc-tion set {(in, c n)|Ai}Nn=1 , where in represents an available instruction for this application and cn

specifies its associated constraint (if applicable). Following prior work (Lu et al., 2024; Rawles et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024b), each task Tj is represented as a sequence of GUI interaction episodes: 

Tj = {(I, i t, c t, g t, a t)}Tt=1 , (1) 44579 where I denotes the global task instruction, it is the atomic instruction for step t, ct is the constraint, 

gt is the current screenshot, and at is the executed GUI action. Notably, an atomic instruction it may internally correspond to multiple low-level actions and screenshots, while still being treated as a single high-level instruction in our formulation. 

2.2 Atomic Instruction and Constraints 

Prior mobile GUI benchmarks often scale task instructions through template substitution or lin-early concatenation, which limits semantic diver-sity and overlooks application-specific constraints (e.g., “share via social media” is a generic ac-tion reused across apps regardless of their distinct functionalities). To address these limitations, we design a structured, multi-stage pipeline that inte-grates human knowledge, LLMs augmentation and rigorous filtering. As illustrated in Fig 2(a), the pipeline consists of: 1) Human Seeding : annota-tors curate a seed set of executable atomic instruc-tions for each application Ai, capturing its core user interactions. 2) LLM-Augmentation : to ex-pand beyond the handcrafted set, multiple LLMs (e.g., GPT-4 , Claude , DeepSeek ) are prompted with the application name, app-store description and seed instructions to iteratively generate addi-tional atomic instructions aligned with the app’s functionality. 3) Constraint Induction : for each atomic instruction, LLMs propose up to 3 candidate constraints grounded in application context. Con-straints are categorized into a compact taxonomy, including numeric ranges (e.g., “price within bud-get” ), thresholds (e.g., “rating ≥ 4.5”), boolean attributes (e.g., “free shipping” ), temporal condi-tions (e.g., set the event start time to 3:00 p.m. ) and preference-based filters (e.g., “find news related to Sports” ). 4) Filtering and Verification : auto-mated LLM-based pre-filtering removes infeasible or redundant outputs, followed by manual verifica-tion to ensure executability, realism and diversity. Instructions that differ only by trivial slot-filling entities or minimal lexical variation are discarded (e.g., “search for Sports news” and “find news re-lated to Technology” are treated as duplicates at the atomic instruction level), and only semantically distinct instructions are accepted. This pipeline produces a diverse library of atomic instructions enriched with application-specific constraints, lay-ing a solid foundation for constructing subsequent graph-structured compositional tasks. 

2.3 Compositional Tasks Construction 

Building on the atomic instruction library, we con-struct Graph-structured Compositional Tasks 

that better approximate common mobile interac-tion patterns. Each task is internally represented as a directed graph G = ( V, E), where nodes v ∈ V 

are atomic instructions and edges e ∈ E are con-straints that specify valid transitions. These con-straints include the application-specific conditions introduced in Section 2.2, GUI state (e.g., stock availability, rating), contextual signals (e.g, time, weather), as well as necessary predicates or depen-dencies that govern task progression. 

Graph Structures. As illustrated in Fig 2(b), we instantiate four compositional task structures for atomic-instruction-centric mobile GUI tasks: 1) Conjunctive : multiple sub-constraints that must all be satisfied, with flexible execution order. 2) 

Sequential : a chain of atomic instructions forming a long-horizon task. 3) Conditional : a task with a binary decision node and two alternative branches (if/else). 4) Hierarchical : a multi-level decision structure formed by nesting conditional branches, simulating user’s fallback and adaptive behaviors. 

Task Construction. For each application Ai

with its atomic instruction set {(in, c n)|Ai}Nn=1 ,we first sample a graph structure together with de-sired task length and branching depth. A reasoning LLM (Team et al., 2023; Anthropic, 2025; Liu et al., 2024a) is then prompted to select and bind atomic instruction nodes, synthesize constraints for edges, and generate a coherent global natural-language instruction I that describes the overall task. 

Quality Control. We observe that different LLMs exhibit complementary behaviors during task construction: GPTs (Achiam et al., 2023) ex-cel at coherent composition but may introduce overly rigid or unnatural decision rules (e.g., “fol-low a post if likes are odd, else comment” ); 

Deepseek (Liu et al., 2024a) produces detailed ra-tionales but often yields shallow rearrangements lacking sequential logic; Gemini (Team et al., 2023) generates more consistent sequential and branching workflows with realistic decision rules. To improve task diversity and robustness, we adopt a mixture-of-generators strategy that sam-ples from multiple LLMs during task construction. All generated candidate tasks are then screened through LLM-based checking for feasibility and co-herence, followed by human verification to remove trivial variations and ensure semantic diversity. The 44580 Figure 2: Illustration of DAC-Bench construction pipeline. 

resulting tasks yields a curated set of decision-aware, compositional task instructions that form the core of DAC-Bench and support fine-grained evaluation in subsequent experiments. 

2.4 Dataset Annotation Setup. We employed 20 trained annotators to la-bel compositional mobile GUI task instructions. Each annotator was provided with the complete 

high-level instruction and asked to decompose it into step-by-step actions. At each step, annotators produce a corresponding low-level (step-level) in-struction, describing the specific action taken on the current screen. Following the protocol of prior work (Li et al., 2024b; Lu et al., 2024), this schema enables separate evaluation of agent performance at both high-level and low-level granularity. An-notations were conducted on Android and iOS de-vices using physical phones, and interactions are recorded through a customized annotation tool that exports data in a unified JSON format. 

Annotation. For each step within a task episode, annotators recorded the screenshot, low-level (step-level) instruction, action type, and action param-eters. Action types were restricted to a prede-fined set: {Click, Scroll, Type, Navigate to Home, Navigate to Previous Page, Long Press, Complete }. Metadata including episode IDs, device information, and dimensions were auto-matically logged. More details are in Appendix C. 

Quality Control. To ensure realism, annota-tors may flag a task as “IMPOSSIBLE” if it vio-lates application usage conventions or could not be completed as instructed. All annotated data then underwent a multi-stage review process: two inde-pendent proofreaders and co-authors cross-checked all entries to correct residual errors and verify task consistency and executability.

2.6 Evaluation Metric 

Prior mobile GUI benchmarks commonly adopt evaluation metrics such as Exact Match (EM), 

Type Match (TM), Success Rate (SR) and Goal Progress (GP). EM (Li et al., 2024b) requires both agent’s predicted action type and parameters to match the ground truth, while TM only checks the action type. SR measures whether all steps in an episode are executed correctly, but collapses into a binary outcome that is often very low for long instructions and fails to indicate which specific ac-tions caused failure. GP measures the fraction of consecutive correct steps from the beginning of the task, but cannot capture branching structures. While effective for short, sequential tasks, these metrics provide limited resolution for evaluat-ing long-horizon progress and decision-making at branching nodes, which are common in our compo-sitional mobile GUI tasks. 3.2 Main Results 

Tab 2 summarizes the overall performance of eval-uated agents on DAC-Bench. We observe a clear difficulty gradient: both SR and GP decrease as task structures become more complex. On the most complex Hierarchical subset, the average SR falls to 14.00% even under low-level setting, and GP averages merely 13.10% under high-level setting. Among evaluated agents, AGUVIS-7B achieves the highest 89.81% overall EM under low-level setting and AgentCPM-GUI-7B with the highest 53.31% EM under high-level setting. Notably,InfiGUI-R1-3B-thinking exhibits the smallest degradation (2.38%) from Conjunctive/Sequential to Condi-tional/Hierarchical, indicating strongest robustness to task length and structural variation. 

Comparison with prior benchmarks. To ver-ify that the increased difficulty of DAC-Bench is not an artifact of data construction (e.g., artificially extended step descriptions or redundant GUI el-ements), we include a Single Atomic Instruction subset as control group. Agent performance on this subset is comparable to that on prior single-step benchmarks (81.19% on ours v.s. 80.81% on An-droidControl (Li et al., 2024b), a widely used mo-bile GUI benchmark), confirming that DAC-Bench preserves the feasibility of basic action execution. To further examine differences beyond task statistics alone, we compare agent performance on DAC-Bench with that on widely used mobile GUI benchmarks. Averaged across task structures, DAC-Bench results in an accuracy drop of 20.69% rela-tive to prior datasets. In contrast to sequential-only benchmarks such as AndroidControl, DAC-Bench places greater emphasis on structural correctness: under high-level instructions, EM is 24.69% lower than AndroidControl, and even AndroidControl’s hardest split remains 26.29% higher than the Condi-tional/Hierarchical subsets of DAC-Bench. These results indicate that DAC-Bench introduces ad-ditional difficulty from long-horizon, decision-aware task structures , while maintaining compa-rable performance on atomic instruction execution. 

3.3 Ablation Studies High-level v.s. Low-level Instructions. Tab 2 compares agent performance under low-level (step-specified) and high-level (goal-only) instructions. Across all agents, EM/TM are consistently higher with low-level guidance, indicating that most agents can reliably execute explicitly grounded actions. When shifted to high-level instructions, TM drops by 24.11% on average and EM falls below 32.78%, revealing a gap between current agents’ strong visual grounding/navigation and weak global task reasoning. In real-world scenar-ios, users rarely give perfectly disambiguated step instructions making this gap even more apparent as task complexity increases. 

Impact of Task Length. Under low-level set-ting (Tab. 2), TM/EM remains relatively stable as task length increases, since each step is explicitly specified. In contrast, high-level setting exhibit strong length sensitivity (Fig. 3): GP decreases sharply with longer sequences, falling below 20% once tasks exceed 6-8 steps. 3.4 Case studies and Error Analysis Q1. Why do agents fail on complex tasks? 

We analyze representative failure cases using 

InfiGUI-R1-3B , which provides access to interme-diate reasoning traces. We observe that agents’ fail-ure modes fall into 2 major categories: perception-related errors and reasoning-related errors .Perception-related errors reveal agents’ visual grounding limitations , rather than purely “reading” failures caused by Optical Character Recognition (OCR). Notably, in many cases agents can correctly read decision-relevant signals (numbers, labels, tog-gle states), but still fail to translate them into the correct interaction. Four main failure modes are ob-served: Mode 1: UI affordance misunderstanding. 

Agents may misinterpret which UI element affords the intended operation (e.g., clicking the article title instead of “remove from favorites” button). 

Mode 2: Off-screen/hidden UI access failures. 

Agents sometimes fail to infer that actions such as Scroll/Swipe/Long Press are needed to reveal miss-ing buttons/apps, resulting in repeated interactions with visible but irrelevant components. Mode 3: Localization and precision errors. In some cases despite the correct intent, the action type is cor-rect but localization is imprecise (e.g., tapping near small or densely packed targets), leading to fail-ures that are not captured by type-level correctness alone. Mode 4: Termination and final-state infer-ence errors. Agents may prematurely conclude that a task is complete or fail to recognize completion (e.g., assuming a TV show is already added to “My List” and skipping the subsequent required steps). This often results in not issuing Complete/Stop and entering repetitive loops in long-horizon tasks. As illustrated in Fig 5, reasoning-related errors reveal agents’ decision-making weakness . These errors mainly arise from decision execution rather than generic reasoning difficulty. Two recurring failure modes are observed. Mode 1: Ignoring branching constraints. The agent often defaults 44584 Figure 4: Visualization of W-LCS scores as a function of task step length.

In some cases, the agent produces a coherent reasoning trace that acknowl-edges both the instruction and GUI state, but subse-quently executes an action that contradicts with its own reasoning, again defaulting to the first option. These failure patterns highlight a discrepancy be-tween strong step-level execution and weak condi-tional decision-making: while agents achieve near-saturated TM/EM on Single, short Sequential and low-level settings, they struggle when required to compare multiple candidate actions against global instructions and on-screen conditions. One con-tributing factor is the limited exposure of current mobile GUI agents to decision-oriented task struc-tures during training, which constrains their ability to reliably handle branching decisions.

Action Type Effects. Tab 4 shows substantial performance variation across action types. Long Press , a realistic yet underrepresented action, ex-hibits the lowest accuracy across agents (on aver-age 66.31% lower than Click /Type ). For UI-TARS and OS-Atlas, the Complete action underperforms other agents by 52.03%, indicating their difficulty in recognizing task termination in long-horizon settings. Their overall SR rises by 8.18% when 

Complete is excluded, suggesting that many fail-ures stem from termination misrecognition rather than execution errors. Annotators also noted the near absence of Double-Tap : although often re-placeable by alternative actions, its omission from most agents’ action space and training data may limit generalization. These observations highlight the impact of action space coverage and final-state inference on agent reliability. 

History Window Length. Long-horizon tasks place increased demands on historical context management. We observe that the optimal his-tory length varies across agents. For instance, 

AgentCPM achieves its highest high-level accuracy with a 4-step history window (1.38% higher than full-history or no-history). However, its low-level accuracy declines as history grows, indicating that longer context is not uniformly beneficial. In con-trast, AGUVIS benefits from longer histories under high-level instructions but shows limited sensitiv-ity under low-level settings. We identify this as 44585 a trade-off among multiple factors: 1) Contex-tual Overload : excessively long textual/histories may increase confusion and accumulative error carry-over. 2) Visual Down-sampling : aggregat-ing many screenshots reduces effective resolution, harming precision actions such as small UI target clicks. 3) Efficiency : longer histories increase in-ference latency, problematic for real-time mobile scenarios. These results suggest that effective his-tory utilization depends on model design rather than context length alone. 

Instruction Language and Platform. We fur-ther analyze performance across instruction lan-guages and platforms (more results in Appendix G and F). Qwen2.5-VL-7B demonstrates greater ro-bustness to English–Chinese instruction shifts, even achieving 3.5% higher EM on Chinese instruc-tions. In addition, we observe that InfiGUI-R1-3B 

series benefits from its “thinking” prompting, ef-fectively reduces outputs formatting errors. Across agents, high-level instructions yield more irregular outputs than low-level ones, and Chinese prompts are generally more error-prone than English. For platform difference, since most mobile GUI train-ing corpora skewed toward Android, average per-formance drops by 5.30% on iOS, likely due to GUI styling and action differences. 

Q3. What actionable directions does DAC-Bench suggest for future agents? Based on the above diagnosis, we suggest several targeted direc-tions for future mobile GUI agents: (i) decision-node-focused pre-training to improve branch se-lection and constraint-to-action binding (consis-tent with the gains we observe from decision-node-only GRPO), (ii) rare-action and visibility train-ing to reliably execute Scroll/Swipe/Long Press and reveal hidden controls, (iii) localization cal-ibration for small UI targets under varying res-olutions and layouts (especially same app across Android and iOS platforms), and (iv) improved termination/final-state inference to reduce repeti-tive loops and trigger Complete/Stop appropriately. 

4 Conclusion 

In this paper, we presented DAC-Bench , a bench-mark designed to eveluate mobile GUI agents on long-horizon and decision-aware task execu-tion. By constructing compositional tasks with sequential, conjunctive, conditional, and hierarchi-cal structures, DAC-Bench extends beyond prior benchmarks that focus primarily on short, linear workflows. Experimental results show that, while current agents perform reliably on single-step ac-tions, their performance degrades substantially on longer tasks and branching decisions, revealing limitations not captured by existing evaluation set-tings. We hope DAC-Bench can serve as a diag-nostic benchmark and facilitate future progress in decision-aware mobile GUI agent design.

Limitation 

DAC-Bench follows an offline, static episode-replay paradigm: each task is evaluated on recorded screenshots paired with a human-executed trajec-tory. This design enables controllable and repro-ducible comparisons across agents, but it does not fully capture challenges in online interactive environments, such as non-stationary in-app con-tent, system interruptions (notifications/permission prompts), or other stochastic dynamics (in-app ad-vertisement and banners). Online evaluation is a complementary paradigm for testing robustness un-der deployment-like conditions, and we plan to explore it as a future extension. Our primary evaluation compares agents against a single reference (gold) trajectory per episode, which may undercount alternative yet valid strate-gies in real apps (e.g., search-first vs. category navigation). We mitigate severe ambiguity through quality control and report progress-sensitive met-rics (e.g., W-LCS) to reduce false negatives from minor deviations, but this limitation is not fully eliminated. As future work, we plan to incorpo-rate multiple reference trajectories (or strategy-level equivalence classes) and complementary goal-state/end-state checks to better account for valid behavioral diversity. We acknowledge that although DAC-Bench covers diverse application categories, platforms 44586 (Android/iOS), and bilingual instructions (En-glish/Chinese), it remains limited in cultural and linguistic scope, including more device brands and models (e.g., Mobile Tablets and iPads), more ap-plication categories and comparison of same appli-cation under different langue settings (e.g., Chinese applications may contain advertisements compared with their English versions). Future extensions may address these limitations to ensure broader fairness and inclusiveness. We consider actively incorporat-ing more downstream application categories such as Medical, Education and Games. We also acknowledge that current workflows are human-LLM approximation of real-world user actions, and may not fully reflect all user cases and complex decisions. Additionally, although we allow annotators mark some tasks as “IMPOSSI-BLE”, ambiguous or conflicting tasks may still ex-ist in real user requests and our dataset has limited coverage for such scenarios. Despite employing 20 professional annotators and a dual-review process, a small number of am-biguous or unclear low-level instructions may still exist. The linguistic diversity of high-level instruc-tions remains limited (ambiguous, colloquial phras-ing characteristic of real users), with most main-taining relatively standardized expressions. We did not systematically verify potential over-lap between DAC-Bench and other agents’ training data in this work (although our data are unique by paper submission), so we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that a small number of task patterns appeared in other agents’ training.

Prior efforts in mobile GUI benchmark construction can be grouped into three following paradigms. 1) Human Handicraft Designed Instructions: AITW (Rawles et al., 2023) categorizes tasks based on the number of action steps into multiple-step and single-step categories. Multiple-step tasks are obtained through human annotation, technical documentation, and LLM-enhanced instructions, while single-step tasks are derived by extracting shorter action sequences from longer action sequences. AITZ (Zhang et al., 2024b), built upon AITW, further filtered out incorrect or mismatched instructions and utilized GPTs to assist in proofreading task instruction semantic descriptions. Other mainstream datasets and benchmarks (Rawles et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2022; Li et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2024b; Lee et al., 2024) widely adopted such human written instructions to simulate real-world user cases by either crowd sourcing or mimicking daily user case and routine. 2) Extracted and generated from large-scale dataset or website (Liu et al., 2025a; Chai et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b): Android-arena (Xing et al., 2024) identify webpage and descriptions related to application functionality through web retrieval, then store these software descriptions in a vector database, and finally utilize an LLM to reconstruct app tasks instructions from them. 3) Expand based on existing instructions and template replacement: GUI-Odyssey (Lu et al., 2024) through manually brainstorming instruction task templates, through template substituting the application name or action description, quickly expand current instruction to a large-scale dataset and finally rewrite with GPT-4. SPA-Bench (Chen et al., 2024) first start with single-step instruction and through expanding one action at a time to obtain long chain instruction and finally achieve difficulty rising. Despite these efforts, existing benchmarks exhibit limitations: heavy reliance on human annotation or template substitution, limited instruction diversity, and a bias toward chain-like workflows. Consequently, they fall short of simulating the decision-making and judgment-based behaviors seen in real user operations, which highlights our design principles. 

B Appendix: Use of LLMs 

Our LLM usage are stated in Section 2 and concluded as below. Instruction Augmentation : we utilized multiple LLMs for expanding human seed atomic instructions, collecting constraints and iteratively refinement with human collaboration. Complex Instruction Construction : we utilized multiple LLMs for construction longer instructions from atomic actions, iterative enhancing and quality checking (e.g., grammar, coherence, feasibility). We ensured that all LLM generated instructions are double verified by either paper co-authors or annotators to prohibit usage/inappropriate instructions, with essential manual re-writing. The experiments related to mobile GUI agents are conducted with LLMs, all models (checkpoints, configurations and licenses) are obtained from their official open-source implementation without additional training or modification. We did not use LLM-generated contents for paper writing, but merely grammar checking.
