Abstract 

Mobile GUI agents are typically fine-tuned 7B-scale vision-language models (VLMs) that generate each action as text. However, these VLMs decode every action token by token, and even agents that score candidate actions keep a large language model or VLM in the loop, making them slow and memory-hungry. To ad-dress these issues, we propose GUI-JEPA, to our knowledge the first decoder-free GUI agent policy built on a Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA), which selects actions in the latent space of a JEPA encoder pretrained on unlabelled GUI screenshots. Its action- and region-conditioned latent predictor turns the screen latent into one latent per candidate ac-tion, letting lightweight heads score all candi-dates against the instruction in a single batched pass, without decoding a token. We further show that the scorer relies on this latent path-way and that the predictor is trained for the decision itself: a randomly initialised predictor does just as well as one pretrained on screen dy-namics. On AndroidControl, GUI-JEPA is 2.8– 4.0 × faster on the same GPU than a Qwen2.5-VL-7B agent we fine-tune with the same inputs, while reaching 86–92% of its in-distribution ac-curacy with 6% of its parameters. Code is avail-able at https://github.com/bcwang-sjtu/ GUI-JEPA .

Yet in GUI grounding a VLM fixes its target elements during prefill, with decoding only refining coordinates (Lin et al., 2026a), and decoding on the critical path makes agents correct but late (Dong et al., 2026). Could a GUI agent score its actions in the latent space of a joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA; Assran et al., 2023), without decoding them at all? GUI world models generate pixels, code or text and serve an LLM policy as simulator or verifier (Luo et al., 2026; Zheng et al., 2026; Mei et al., 2026; Cao et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026b), or as a pretraining target (Liu et al., 2026b). On GUIs, JEPAs have so far served understanding and ver-ification: UI-JEPA describes user intent through an LLM decoder (Fu et al., 2025), LGWM verifies GUI transitions (Zhang et al., 2026a), and next-frame feature prediction regularises the training of a VLM agent (Yang et al., 2026b). Candidate scorers over the accessibility tree (a11y) keep an LLM or VLM in the loop (Dai et al., 2026; Zhang, 1arXiv:2610.36877v1 [cs.AI] 27 Oct 2026 2026; Christianos et al., 2025), and Agentic-JEPA, which acts with a JEPA, is text-only with 0% out-of-distribution (OOD) success (Rodrigo-Ginés, 2026). To our knowledge, no GUI agent has yet used a JEPA as the policy that selects its actions. We bring JEPAs from perception and robotics to GUI control, moving action selection from the decoder into the latent space of a frozen JEPA en-coder. GUI-JEPA encodes the screenshot once with a JEPA ViT-L pretrained on GUI screenshots, trans-forms that latent once per candidate (a11y element or global action) with a 39.9M residual predictor conditioned on the candidate’s action and region of interest (ROI), scores the candidate-conditioned latents with a cosine structural head, adds a 33M text-matcher score and takes the argmax, all in one batched pass (Figure 2). As in earlier candidate-scoring agents, the candidates come from the a11y tree, but GUI-JEPA never generates a token, so the cost of a step depends on how many candidates it scores. Under the same inputs, judge and GPU as a Qwen2.5-VL-7B agent that we fine-tune with LoRA (SFT), GUI-JEPA is 2.8–4.0 × faster and reaches 92.4% / 86.1% of its in-distribution (IDD) Low / High accuracy with 6.0% of its parameters and 4.6–6.4 × less GPU memory (Figure 1). Our contributions are: • GUI-JEPA , the first GUI agent whose pol-icy is built on a JEPA. It selects actions by scoring candidate-conditioned JEPA latents, with no decoder, and is 2.8–4.0 × faster than a same-protocol 7B agent that decodes token by token. • Adapting a JEPA policy to GUIs: a11y candidates as actions, ROI- and action-conditioned residual prediction, a structural head whose only candidate-dependent vi-sual input is the conditioned latent, and two text rankers that together add 3.8 out-of-distribution AC-High points (Table 2). • Mechanism: the scorer relies on the JEPA la-tent pathway, which is trained for the decision. A randomly initialised predictor matches a dynamics-pretrained one, so a JEPA policy for GUIs needs no dynamics pretraining. 

2 Related Work 

VLM GUI agents and their cost. The dominant mobile GUI agent is a VLM of 7B+ parameters that emits an action token by token from a screenshot (Wu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Qin et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025; Bai et al., 2025); 3B agents re-port AndroidControl under incompatible protocols (Table 11). On-device agents need seconds per step (Park et al., 2026), and efficiency work cheapens the decoder (Bai et al., 2026); GUI-JEPA removes it altogether. 

Discriminative and candidate-scoring GUI agents. Closest in input are the a11y-text PaLM 2S baselines of AndroidControl (Sec-tion 4.2). LiMAC selects UI elements from screen-shots and UI trees with a small action transformer, calls Florence-2 (Xiao et al., 2024) for text argu-ments and reports its own metric (Christianos et al., 2025); V-Droid verifies a11y candidates with a prefill-only LLM (Dai et al., 2026), Jev-Mobile se-lects a11y identifiers with a typed decision model (Zhang, 2026), and IntentScore verifies a genera-tive policy’s actions (Chen et al., 2026); each keeps a generative LLM or VLM in the loop. GUI-JEPA shares a11y enumeration and argmax but scores a candidate-conditioned JEPA latent plus a 33M text matcher, with no generative model in the loop. 

World models for GUI agents. GUI world mod-els predict the next state as pixels (Luo et al., 2026; Ning et al., 2026), code (Zheng et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a), text (Chae et al., 2025; Mei et al., 2026) or textual element sketches (Cao et al., 2026) and serve an LLM policy as simulator, verifier or data synthesiser; none of the four output modalities compared by Xu et al. (2026b) is joint-embedding. STP pretrains forward and inverse GUI dynamics in a unified multimodal model that still decodes actions (Liu et al., 2026b), and Li et al. (2026) train a generative text world model over a11y-tree states by predicted-state matching, whose predic-tions feed a separate reward model that ranks can-didate actions. GUI-JEPA is decoder-free and is 

the policy, trained, like that web world model, for how it is used.

3 Method: GUI-JEPA 

GUI-JEPA chooses each action by scoring candi-dates. At every step it enumerates the executable actions from the a11y tree, embeds the screenshot once with a frozen JEPA encoder, transforms that screen latent once per candidate with a residual predictor, scores each candidate-conditioned latent and the candidate’s text against the text condition, and executes the highest-scoring candidate (Fig-ure 2). Since no token, pixel or line of code is generated, a decision is one batched forward pass whose cost grows with the number of candidates. 

Problem and notation. We learn a step-level of-fline policy on AndroidControl (Li et al., 2024) that predicts the recorded action from one step’s inputs and the ground-truth history. A step has a screen-shot xt, an a11y tree Tt, a text condition g (the goal in AC-High, the step instruction in AC-Low) and the ground-truth history ht (the previous action vector and the mean latent change from the previ-ous frame; the text matcher receives the previous actions as text). A frozen encoder gives the screen latent, patch tokens zt = fθ(xt) ∈ R196 ×1024 . The candidate set Ct = enumerate( Tt) ∪ G joins ele-ment candidates (a click per enumerated node, an 

input_text per editable one) with a fixed set G of global actions. A candidate c carries a 16-d action vector ac (type one-hot plus coordinates or scroll 3direction) and, if it has an element, its region roi( c)

(global actions: zero region feature, whole-screen pooling). The policy picks ˆct = arg max c∈C t s(c).

3.1 Input step and candidates 

Element-level candidates are the visible a11y nodes flagged clickable, long-clickable, editable or scrol-lable, capped at the 80 smallest by area, which keeps specific elements and bounds the batch; each carries the text of the node and its descendants, its bounding box and its type. Every enumerated node yields a click candidate, and every editable node also an input_text candidate whose text is filled in by the span ranker (Section 3.4). Global actions act on the whole screen: the four scroll di-rections, navigate_back , navigate_home , wait 

and one open_app , whose argument is the longest training-vocabulary app name in the text condition. The ground-truth element of a click is the smallest enumerated candidate that contains the recorded click point (details and coverage: Section B).

3.3 Residual latent predictor 

The predictor gϕ (39.9M) is a JEPA-style action-conditioned latent predictor that maps the screen latent and one candidate to a candidate-conditioned latent. Its action input concatenates ac with the ROI-pooled feature uc = pool roi( c)(zt) ∈ R1024 

of the candidate’s element (zero for global actions), so that two candidates of the same type, such as two buttons, are told apart by what their regions show as well as by their coordinates. An MLP of this pair is added to a learned query token, and a three-layer transformer attends over the 196 patch tokens and the query, giving the candidate-conditioned la-tent ˜zct = zt + ∆( zt, a c, uc). The residual form keeps the content of the current screen and lets the predictor express only a candidate-specific change, which preserves the detail needed to ground an el-ement. All candidates of a step form one batch. 

gϕ is initialised from a dynamics predictor trained on AndroidControl transitions to predict the next step’s latent from zt and the recorded action (Sec-tion D) and is then fine-tuned jointly with the scor-ing heads. Policy training adds no next-latent loss, and the policy loss alone shapes ˜zct into a decision feature (Section 5.1).

4 Experiments 

4.1 Setup Benchmark and splits. We test on the four offi-cial AndroidControl subsplits (Li et al., 2024): IDD (721 episodes) and the overlapping app_unseen (631), task_unseen (803) and category_unseen (700, contained in task_unseen). The full test set (1,543 episodes, 8,444 steps) and the OOD union (full test minus IDD, 4,586 steps) are the units of ev-idence. IDD results cover 716 of the 721 episodes (3,858 of 3,897 steps), because five IDD episodes also in OOD subsplits count as OOD (Section B). The 137-episode validation split selected the click reranker’s variant, weight β and gate. Systems that state their split report all test subsets together, so we compare them with our full-test result and use IDD only against published IDD numbers. 

Protocol. AC-Low conditions on the step instruc-tion and AC-High on the episode goal, both with the ground-truth action history. Our exact-match judge keeps the original benchmark’s click rule (the chosen candidate’s centre lies inside the ground-truth element box) and departs from the original protocol in four places: it counts no equivalent 

open_app or navigation actions, requires the nor-malised typed text to match, accepts a click for a ground-truth long_press (9 IDD steps), and takes the ground-truth box from the smallest enumerated candidate (originally the smallest a11y node), scor-ing clicks without a candidate (1.6–2.6%) as errors where the original discards them (Section B). Can-didates come from the a11y tree, a privileged input that pixel-only agents do not use. 

4.2 Main results Closest published protocol. The AndroidCon-trol paper’s LoRA-tuned PaLM 2S over a11y text (Li et al., 2024, Tab. 5) shares our a11y input (we add the screenshot), click rule and subsplits, under the original relaxed judge. In distribution, GUI-JEPA matches it on AC-Low (84.06 vs 83.2) and trails on AC-High (60.99 vs 70.8; Tables 6 and 7). 

Exact-match VLM agents. The pixel-only 7–8B exact-match entries of Table 1 decode their actions with 14–16 × more (nominal) parameters than GUI-JEPA. On AC-Low our full-test 78.64 is above OS-Atlas-7B (67.3), MIRAGE-4B (77.6) and Qwen3-VL-8B (77.66), and none of these agents reports a comparable per-step latency. 

Examples. Figure 5 shows GUI-JEPA on three real IDD AC-High steps, one per action kind: it picks a menu entry among 15 candidates, opens the app named in the goal, and types a book title copied from the goal, each with one forward pass.

Speed. The final GUI-JEPA pipeline (base pol-icy, span ranker and click reranker) needs a median 74 ms per step in fp32 and 51.5 ms with bf16 auto-cast, against 207 ms for the 7B agent with vLLM and 507 ms with HF transformers (merged LoRA; Table 3). GUI-JEPA is thus 2.8 × (fp32) to 4.0 ×

Where the time goes. Figure 3 splits the mean step time on the same 100 steps. Decoding takes 51% (vLLM) to 60% (HF) of the 7B agent’s step, on top of its prefill, and the vLLM prefill alone outlasts a whole fp32 GUI-JEPA step, so the gain comes from both the smaller model and the ab-sence of decoding. About half of a GUI-JEPA step goes to the batched predictor and head, which bf16 speeds up threefold, and its cost grows with the number of candidates (Section I). 

4.6 Error analysis 

Clicks, 63% of the steps, account for 5.8 of the 9.9-point AC-High gap to the 7B agent, shared between choosing the click type and grounding the element, the part the text matcher carries (Section 3.4). On global actions the gap lies mainly in the type de-cision, and on open_app and input_text in the argument (per type: Table 15). The two systems also err on different steps (Figure 10): at least one of them is right on 78.7% of IDD AC-High steps, and the margin between GUI-JEPA’s top two scores flags its errors (AUROC 0.74), so uncertain steps can be routed to a same-protocol 7B agent where accuracy matters more than speed (Section J). 

5 Analysis 

5.1 What the latent pathway does Reliance. We lesion the trained base policy at evaluation time, text matcher untouched (Figure 4a; Table 5). On IDD, shuffling the action vectors across all candidates costs 27.2 Low / 23.4 High, shuffling them within a type 11.2 / 10.8 (mostly scroll directions; about 5 High on clicks), and re-placing the candidate-conditioned latent by the cur-rent latent (no predictor) 24.9 / 14.0, mostly on the global actions, which share the full-screen ROI. The action vector reaches the structural head only through the predictor, and training cannot route around it. Retrained at full scale with the identity map in place of the predictor, the policy reaches 62.36 / 42.33, 21.4 / 14.5 points below the base policy, again on the global actions, which the iden-tity map leaves indistinguishable (Table 8). The pathway thus carries chiefly action type and scroll direction, and also helps select among clicks. 

The pathway is trained for the decision. A ran-domly initialised predictor trained with the identi-cal full-scale recipe does as well as the dynamics-pretrained one (+0.50 High / −1.01 Low; Fig-ure 4c), and predictive auxiliary objectives (per-patch (Sun et al., 2026), pooled, multi-horizon, LeWorldModel-style (Maes et al., 2026)) add noth-ing at 4K-episode scale (Table 8). The predictor thus learns what the decision needs, and forward-model probes show that it forecasts the next latent worse than a copy of the current one (Section D). It acts as an action- and ROI-conditioned transform of the current screen, so a JEPA policy for GUIs needs no dynamics pretraining. This agrees with findings that predictability and action relevance come apart (Gu et al., 2026) and with training world models for the decision (Li et al., 2026).

The structural head makes the policy use the predictor. 6 Conclusion 

GUI-JEPA, to our knowledge the first GUI agent whose policy is built on a JEPA, replaces ac-tion decoding with one batched scoring pass over candidate-conditioned JEPA latents. With 0.50B parameters it needs 51.5–74 ms per step, 2.8–4.0 ×

less than a vLLM-served 7B agent on the same inputs, and reaches 92.4% / 86.1% of its IDD Low / High accuracy with 6.0% of its parameters. The JEPA latent pathway is essential to the scorer (lesions cost 10.8–23.4 IDD High points) and is trained for the decision: a randomly ini-tialised predictor does as well as a dynamics-pretrained one. A JEPA used as a GUI action scorer should thus be judged by the decisions it supports, not by next-latent fidelity, and can be trained for them directly. Extending such scorers from steps to tasks is the next step for JEPA-based GUI agents. 

Limitations 

GUI-JEPA consumes the a11y tree, a privileged structured input that pixel-only agents do not use; the 1.6–2.6% of test clicks without a candidate count as errors, and every latency excludes screen capture and a11y retrieval. The policy is a step-level scorer; online task completion, which needs a termination head and a planner, is left to future work, as is using the predictor as a forward model. Design choices other than the reranker’s variant, weight and gate were made on test IDD and re-checked on validation only for the span ranker and overall base-policy accuracy (Section 4.4). Speed-ups are measured at batch 1 against vLLM and HF serving of the 7B agent (Section 4.5). The same-protocol 7B reference is one LoRA adapter per setting trained on 4,000 of 13,594 episodes. The 8method is specialised to mobile a11y candidates; other candidate sources, such as web DOM nodes, need their own candidate texts and action vectors. Non-JEPA encoders are compared through a probe on the frozen tokens (Section G); a comparison inside the full-scale policy is future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

GUI-JEPA lowers the cost of GUI agents that act on a user’s device. A wrong action, such as sending a message, deleting data or confirming a payment, can be hard to undo, and step-level errors remain common; a deployment needs user confirmation before irreversible actions. The speed that makes on-device agents practical also makes it cheaper to automate interactions meant for humans, such as mass account creation. The policy reads the acces-sibility tree, which can contain personal on-screen data; running the 0.50B model on-device keeps that data local. We use only public datasets under their licences (AndroidControl Apache-2.0, GUI-Odyssey CC-BY-4.0, VideoCUA and ScreenSpot-Pro MIT), collect no new data, and involve no hu-man subjects, annotators or crowd workers.

A Reproducibility statement 

The encoder is a ViT-L/16 at 224 px, initialised from ImageNet weights and trained with a JEPA objective adapted from I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) on 189,262 unlabelled screenshots from GUI-Odyssey (Lu et al., 2025) and VideoCUA (Jian et al., 2026) for 2 epochs ( ≈0.76 GPU-hour on one H20), with no action labels and no AndroidControl data (Sections 3.2 and H). The policy is trained on the 13,594 cached episodes of the 13,604-episode AndroidControl training split (Li et al., 2024) with a listwise cross-entropy, AdamW and 6 epochs, for three seeds (Section 3.5 and Table 13). We evaluate on the official test subsplits with the exact-match judge of Section 4.1 and time both systems on the same 100 real IDD steps on one H20 at batch 1 (Section I). 

B Protocol details 

Splits and overlap. The official 

test_subsplits.json of AndroidControl (Li et al., 2024) lists 721 IDD, 631 app_unseen, 803 task_unseen and 700 category_unseen episodes, whose union is the full test set of 1,543 episodes and 8,444 steps. The subsplits are not disjoint: every category_unseen episode is also a task_unseen episode, and app_unseen shares 606 episodes with category_unseen and 607 with task_unseen. We therefore treat the full test set and the OOD union (full test minus IDD as evaluated: 827 episodes, 4,586 steps) as the units of evidence and report the three OOD subsplits, each evaluated in full, only as a breakdown (Section C). Five of the 721 IDD episodes also appear in OOD subsplits and count as OOD, so every IDD accuracy in the paper is computed on 716 episodes and 3,858 of the 3,897 steps (99.0%); the coverage analysis below uses all 721 episodes. The 137-episode validation split (690 steps) selected the click reranker’s variant, its weight β and its confidence gate. Every other design choice, including both text rankers, was made on test IDD; the validation row of Table 6 checks how these choices carry over. We train on 13,594 of the 13,604 training episodes of Li et al. (2024, Tab. 3) (74,714 of 74,722 steps). 

Candidate enumeration and coverage. 

Element-level candidates are the visible accessibility-tree (a11y) nodes flagged click-able, long-clickable, editable or scrollable, plus containers that inherit clickability, de-duplicated 12 by bounds and capped at the 80 smallest by area; editable nodes also yield input_text 

candidates. Global actions are the four scroll directions, navigate_back , navigate_home ,

wait and one open_app , whose argument is the longest training-vocabulary app name in the goal or instruction. A ground-truth click or long-press is mapped to the smallest enumerated candidate that contains its coordinate, whereas the original benchmark takes the smallest a11y node of any kind; the click rule itself (the chosen candidate’s centre inside the ground-truth box) is unchanged. When no candidate contains the coordinate the step is unmapped and counts as an error for our policy (the original benchmark discards such steps). The mapped fraction is 97.4–98.4% on the test subsplits (IDD 98.0%, 2,405 of 2,454 click and long-press steps; full test 97.6%, 4,961 of 5,083), 98.8% on validation and 98.4% on the training split. An IDD step has 23.6 a11y candidates on average (median 18, p95 57; 22.2 on the full test set and 23.2 on the training split), and the 80-candidate cap is reached on 16 IDD, 22 full-test and 272 training steps. Per-type IDD step counts are in Table 15. 

Judge. A step is correct under our exact-match judge when the predicted action type equals the ground-truth type and the argument matches: a click when the centre of the chosen candidate lies inside the ground-truth element box, which is the click rule of the original benchmark (Li et al., 2024, App. D.3); an input_text when the typed string equals the ground-truth string after normalisation; 

open_app and scroll when the argument matches exactly. The judge departs from the original pro-tocol in four places, in both directions. (i) It is stricter in accepting no equivalent actions, whereas the original protocol treats open_app and a click on an element bearing the app name as equivalent, and likewise navigate_back and a click on the on-screen Back button. (ii) It is more lenient on 

input_text : it compares the normalised text only, whereas the original protocol scores typing as an element action that also requires the target element to lie inside the ground-truth box and the text to be identical. (iii) It is more lenient in accepting a click for a ground-truth long_press (9 IDD steps). (iv) It takes the ground-truth box from our enumer-ated candidates: a mapped click is judged against a box at least as large as the original’s smallest a11y node (more lenient), while an unmapped click counts as an error, where Li et al. (2024, App. D.1) discard it (stricter). On click steps, 62.7% of IDD steps, both judges thus apply the same centre-in-box rule. Exact-match (EM) re-evaluations in the literature are the closest published protocol; num-bers under the original relaxed protocol are not interchangeable with ours. 

Conditioning and seeds. One trained model serves both settings: AC-Low conditions on the step instruction and AC-High on the episode goal; both receive the ground-truth action history. We train three policy seeds. The main text reports seed 3, the best seed on every test split, at every stage and in both settings; full-scale results in this appendix are means ± sd over the three seeds un-less a seed is named, and Tables 4 and 5 list ev-ery seed. For n=3 the 95% t-interval half-width, 

4.30 sd /√3, is about ±2.4 points (IDD High 

±2.37, full-test High ±2.34, IDD Low ±2.52). Seeds capture training variance only, so external comparisons also use episode-clustered bootstrap intervals (Section B.2).

D Mechanism analyses 

Table 8 collects the evidence on the latent-predictor pathway summarised in Section 5.1; the text below adds only what the table does not show. 

Inference-time lesions. The lesions are applied at evaluation time to the trained full-scale base poli-cies (text rankers off), so they measure how much the trained scorer relies on the pathway; the re-trained controls below test whether it is needed. Removing the predictor ( ˜zct := zt) hurts mainly the global actions: of its 21.9 AC-Low / 14.6 AC-High IDD points, 19.0 / 14.1 fall on scroll, open_app and 

navigate_back (19.6 / 15.6 with wait ). These share the full-screen ROI, so without the predictor their action vectors no longer reach the score and the three types are never chosen correctly, while click accuracy barely moves (83.9 to 83.2 Low, 58.4 to 60.6 High). The within-type shuffle, which permutes the four scroll directions, costs 8.6 / 6.3 points on scroll steps and 2.3 / 4.2 on clicks. With-out the text matcher the structural head’s click grounding is 30.9 Low / 31.8 High against 89.8 / 71.8 for the full scorer: the matcher carries most of the click grounding, while the head alone still grounds about a third of the click steps whose type it gets right. The reliance holds on every OOD subsplit; for the main-text model, removing the pre-dictor costs 21.7–23.1 Low / 7.9–9.7 High points there, the within-type shuffle 10.5–11.5 / 8.5–9.0 and the all-candidate shuffle 24.4–25.0 / 15.4–16.7 (means: Table 8). 

Training-time variants. Freezing the dynamics-pretrained predictor costs 2.52 AC-Low / 1.25 AC-High IDD points on average (3.78 / 1.89 for seed 3; Table 5). A randomly initialised predictor trained jointly with the identical full-scale recipe matches the dynamics-pretrained one: its High is higher for every seed (+0.50 to +1.17) and its Low within 1.01 points. At pilot scale, the identity map gives 31.73 Low vs 40.59 for the ROI-conditioned pre-dictor (structural head, no text matcher; Figure 4b), and training on shuffled dynamics with a frozen predictor gives 47.33 / 28.46 vs 62.70 / 38.96. 

Retrained controls. Retrained from scratch at full scale with the identical recipe, the identity map in place of the predictor again never chooses scroll, 

open_app or navigate_back correctly ( −11 .3,

−5.1 and −2.6 Low points), while clicks change by −1.8 Low / +0 .2 High points. The matcher-only policy scores 0–12% on scroll, open_app ,

navigate_back , wait and input_text , and its click grounding is 88.4 / 68.2 against the base policy’s 89.8 / 71.8. A no-predictor control tests whether the predictor is needed at all once the action reaches the score: its structural head re-ceives [pool roi( c)(zt); mean( zt); ac] through an MLP matched in parameters to the predictor and the original projection (41.09M against 39.91M + 1.18M), with matcher, history, type bias and recipe unchanged. It matches the base policy in two of three seeds (Table 5; within 1.4 points on app- and category-unseen) and collapses on clicks in the third (click grounding 48.3 Low against 89.6 for its base seed). Given the action vector directly, a parameter-matched head on the same JEPA latents thus reaches the base policy’s accuracy when its training succeeds, whereas the predictor trains sta-bly in all three seeds; either way the action is scored on the JEPA latent, consistent with the predictor’s role as an action- and ROI-conditioned transform of it (Section 5.1).

Encoder and resolution. Swapping in DINOv2-L features (Oquab et al., 2024) changes the encoder and the resolution (518 px) together, so this com-parison does not isolate the effect of resolution. 

Predictor objectives. Predictive auxiliary losses on the predictor do not help (Table 8, bottom). The leakage-free pooled next-latent MSE has a low mean because one of its three runs collapses; the other two are within 1.3 points of their paired controls. Our SIGReg-style regulariser simplifies SIGReg (Balestriero and LeCun, 2025): where LeJEPA uses the Epps–Pulley statistic, it penalises the mean and variance of 256 random projections of the pooled predicted latents, and it acts on frozen encoder features, whereas LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026) trains end to end.

Probe. G.1 Pretraining–test near-duplicates 

We hashed the 9,987 screenshots of the 1,543 An-droidControl test episodes (pHash and dHash, 64 bits) against the 189,262 pretraining screenshots (118,791 GUI-Odyssey, 70,471 VideoCUA). 58 test frames (0.58%) in 43 episodes have a verified near-duplicate (pHash distance ≤ 8 and normalised cross-correlation ≥ 0.9), all from GUI-Odyssey and showing generic system screens. For calibra-tion, the same pHash threshold matches 35.0% of test frames to the frames of 4,000 AndroidControl training episodes (different episodes of the same benchmark) and 9.5% to the pretraining set; ran-dom pairs have a median distance of 32. Removing the 43 episodes changes final High and Low by 

−0.2 (High 59.90 vs 60.12 on IDD, 52.64 vs 52.85 on the full test). Removing every episode with any pHash match within distance 8 (549 episodes), a criterion that also flags generic layouts, gives 58.74 IDD and 51.14 full-test High. At the app level, 29 app names match exactly and 26% of test episodes touch a package that also appears in GUI-Odyssey, with different devices and tasks and no shared screenshots. Pretraining–test leakage is thus negligible for the reported numbers. 

H Implementation details and hyperparameters 

Table 13 lists every component with its measured parameter count and the settings of the reported models; the paragraphs below add only what the table does not state. 

Encoder. The encoder is the ViT-L/16 of Doso-vitskiy et al. (2021) from the timm library. Where I-JEPA (Assran et al., 2023) uses multi-block masks, our student sees the screenshot with a random half of its patches zeroed and regresses the tokens that the EMA target computes on the full image at the masked positions. The encoder is frozen and its 196 patch tokens per frame are cached; the policy never updates it. 

Predictor, head and matcher. All candidates of a step form one predictor batch. The residual form was chosen in IDD pilots without the text matcher: a predictor that re-synthesises the whole latent erases local detail, lowering AC-Low click grounding to 34.8 against 46.8 for the identity map, and the residual form raises pilot AC-Low from 40.59 to 43.93. At pilot scale (IDD) the structural head’s constraint, that its only candidate-dependent visual input is the candidate-conditioned latent, decides whether the pathway is used: a concat-MLP head over [zt, ˜zct , cand] ignores the predic-tor (within 1.4 AC-Low points), while the struc-tural head gains 8.8 points (Figure 4b; Section E). The conditioning vector uses the frozen CLIP text embedding (Radford et al., 2021). The MiniLM-L12 text matcher (Wang et al., 2020; Reimers and Gurevych, 2019) carries most of the click ground-ing: in the pilot, grounding rose from 37.4 to 70.2 when it was added. The input_text argument is copied as a goal span, which can be correct on at most 81.6% of IDD input_text steps. 

Training. Training skips the 1.6% of training click steps whose ground-truth element is not enu-merated. Neither text ranker changes the action-type decision of the base policy, but both act in AC-Low as well as AC-High (Section C). 

Same-protocol 7B reference. Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct (Bai et al., 2025) (8.29B parameters, mea-sured) is fine-tuned with LoRA (Hu et al., 2022) on 4,000 of the 13,594 cached training episodes for one epoch, with one adapter per setting (3.5 / 4.4 GPU-h for High / Low on one H20); the AC-Low adapter’s prompt adds the step instruction to the goal and shares every other setting, whereas GUI-JEPA serves both settings with one model. It is prompted with the same screenshot (377 im-age tokens; at most 384 visual tokens), the same enumerated candidate list and the same ground-truth history as GUI-JEPA, decodes a JSON action greedily, and is scored by the byte-identical judge on the same 716 IDD episodes.
